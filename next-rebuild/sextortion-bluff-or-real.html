Bluff or Real Threat?

Understanding sextortion threats without panic, shame, or assumptions.

A threat can be a bluff, a real threat, or a mixture of both. You do not need to figure that out alone before protecting yourself.

1. Read: what might be happening?

In a common scam, someone using a fake or misleading profile builds trust, asks for an intimate picture or video, then demands money, more images, sexual acts, or continued contact. They may threaten to send material to your followers, family, friends, school, or workplace.

Some threats are bluffs. A scammer may claim to have hacked your phone, copied your contacts, or recorded you when they have not. They may show a public profile list or a fabricated screenshot to make the threat seem convincing.

Some threats involve real access or material. An offender may actually possess an image, know some of your contacts, or share material. A person may also distribute content to intimidate or punish someone. Never assume a threat is harmless just because many scammers bluff.

Remember: A threat is not proof that the person controls your phone, accounts, contacts, or future. And even if they possess an image, that does not mean they will send it to everyone.

2. Common bluffing pressure tactics

  • “I hacked your phone” without credible evidence of access.
  • Countdowns, urgent deadlines, or escalating payment demands.
  • Images of your public followers list presented as proof they control your contacts.
  • Claims that they will send material to every friend unless you pay immediately.
  • Repeated demands for gift cards, cryptocurrency, transfers, or more images.
  • Threats that change whenever you refuse or delay.

These are warning signs of coercion and manipulation. They do not reliably prove whether the offender has or has not shared anything.

3. What to do right now

Stop engaging

Do not argue, negotiate, pay, send more images, or do what they demand. Paying does not guarantee the threats will stop.

Preserve safe evidence

Keep usernames, profile links, message URLs, dates, payment demands, and transaction details. Do not download, forward, repost, or circulate intimate images.

Report and block

Report the account and messages to the platform. After preserving safe evidence, block the person and tighten account privacy.

Get support

Tell someone you trust. If the person is a minor, involve a safe adult. The person targeted is not to blame.

4. If they claim they have your contacts or hacked your device

  • Do not click their links, install apps, or give remote access.
  • Secure your email first, then change reused passwords from a trusted device.
  • Turn on multifactor authentication and review active sessions and recovery details.
  • Do not trust screenshots alone as proof of a hack or of distribution.
  • If you see evidence of account access, use the service's official recovery process.

5. If an intimate image may be shared

If the image was taken when the person was under 18, NCMEC's Take It Down can help eligible people create a digital fingerprint on their own device to help participating platforms detect and remove copies. Do not upload or send the image to anyone to prove what happened.

Adults depicted in non-consensual intimate images can learn about StopNCII.org. It is not an emergency service and does not guarantee removal everywhere.

For suspected online child sexual exploitation in the United States, report to NCMEC CyberTipline. Adults can also report extortion and cyber-enabled crime to FBI IC3. If someone is in immediate physical danger, call 911.
