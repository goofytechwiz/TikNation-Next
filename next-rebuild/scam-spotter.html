Scam Spotter: Real or Bluff?

Learn how scammers use urgency, fear, fake authority, stolen information, and impersonation to pressure people into paying or giving away access.

The goal is not to guess blindly. Separate what the scammer claims from what you can verify, then take safe action.

How to think like a scam spotter

Stay calm. Verify independently. Don't pay or share codes. A threat can be a bluff, a genuine risk, or a mixture. Don't assume everything is fake—and don't accept a scammer's claims as proof.
Pause

Urgency, countdowns, threats, and “act now” messages are pressure tactics.

Verify

Use the official app, website, phone number, or a separate trusted contact route.

Protect

Don't click unexpected links, send money, share verification codes, or install remote-access tools.

Report

Keep safe evidence, report through the platform or appropriate authority, then block where appropriate.

Read: nine real-world scam scenarios

01 · Email phishing

“Your Facebook account will be deleted in 30 minutes.”

A message says suspicious activity was detected and demands that you click a link to verify your identity. The sender name can be faked, and a lookalike address or urgent deadline is a warning sign.

Safer response: Don't click the message link or enter credentials. Inspect the full sender address and link destination cautiously, but remember that display names and even familiar-looking addresses can be spoofed. Open the official app or type the known website address yourself to check account alerts. Report the email as phishing.

02 · Bank impersonation

“Read me the six-digit code to stop a bank attack.”

A caller knows your name or part of a card number and claims to be from your bank. Caller ID and partial personal details do not prove identity.

Safer response: Never disclose a one-time code, PIN, or password to an unexpected caller. Hang up and call the number on your card or in the official banking app. Never move money to a so-called safe account.

03 · Fake webcam-hack extortion

“I recorded you. Pay Bitcoin or I'll expose you.”

Mass scam emails often claim webcam access and threaten to send a recording. They may include an old password from a data breach to sound convincing. That alone does not prove webcam access.

Safer response: Don't pay or reply. Preserve the message, change any exposed or reused password, secure your email, enable multifactor authentication, review account sessions, and report the extortion. If you find credible evidence of compromise, use official recovery steps.

04 · Delivery text

“Pay $1.99 to release your package.”

Unexpected delivery texts can lead to fake payment pages that steal card details or personal information.

Safer response: Don't tap the link or pay through the text. Check tracking in the carrier's official app or website using a tracking number you already have. In the U.S., suspicious texts can be forwarded to 7726 (SPAM).

05 · Fake prize or giveaway

“You won a new phone. Pay $25 shipping today.”

A surprise prize plus an unexpected fee can be a way to collect money, card details, or personal information. A small fee does not make the offer safe.

Safer response: Don't pay or provide details through the message. Find the organizer's official account and published giveaway rules independently. Report impersonation or fraud.

06 · Gift-card impersonation

“I'm your friend. Buy a $100 gift card and send me the code.”

A scammer may impersonate a friend, boss, family member, or public figure. Gift-card codes are like cash once redeemed.

Safer response: Don't buy or send a code. Verify with the person through a separate known number or in-person contact. If a card was purchased, contact its issuer immediately and keep the receipt.

07 · AI voice cloning

“I need a short voice recording.” Later, a caller sounds like you.

AI can imitate voices from recordings, including public videos. A convincing voice is not proof that a caller is who they claim to be. A voice sample by itself does not automatically unlock a phone or bank account.

Safer response: Be thoughtful about sharing voice samples with strangers. Families can agree on a verification phrase and call back using a known number before sending money or acting on emergencies.

08 · Sextortion

“Subscribe, pay, or I'll send your picture to everyone.”

Some offenders bluff about hacking accounts or having access to contacts. Others really possess an image or may distribute it. A public followers list or screenshot alone does not prove a device or account was hacked.

Safer response: Don't pay, comply, or send more images. Stop engaging, preserve safe non-explicit evidence such as usernames and message details, report and block, secure accounts, and seek trusted support. Never download or forward intimate images of minors. NCMEC Take It Down may help with eligible images taken when the person was under 18; adults can learn about StopNCII.org.

09 · Doxxing and exposure threats

“I know your address and workplace. Pay $300 or I'll publish them.”

Names, phone numbers, addresses, and workplaces may come from public records, social media, or data breaches. That does not automatically mean the sender hacked your device. But a credible threat to expose private information or cause physical harm should be taken seriously.

Safer response: Don't pay. Save the threat and sender details, report it, tighten privacy settings, remove exposed information where possible, and tell someone you trust. If there is an immediate threat of physical harm, contact emergency services.

Knowledge check · 9 questions

Choose the safest response. This is an educational self-check, not a certification.

1. An email says your account will be deleted unless you click a link. What should you do?
2. An unexpected caller asks for the six-digit code sent to your phone.
3. An email claims it recorded you through your webcam and demands Bitcoin. What is true?
4. A delivery text asks for a small redelivery fee through a link.
5. A surprise giveaway asks for shipping money to claim a phone.
6. A friend messages asking for gift-card codes.
7. A voice sounds exactly like a family member asking for emergency money.
8. Someone threatens to expose an intimate image unless you pay.
9. Someone threatens to publish your address and workplace unless you pay.

Remember: Scammers want you rushed, frightened, or embarrassed. Pause, verify through a separate trusted route, protect your accounts, and report. If a person is in immediate danger, contact emergency services.
