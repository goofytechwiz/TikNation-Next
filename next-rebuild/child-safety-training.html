Safety comes before investigation

Advocates help by recognizing concerning behavior, reporting it through safe channels, and supporting young people. They do not run stings, impersonate children, solicit confessions, confront suspects, or investigate independently.

Never create or request sexual content involving a minor. Do not download, screenshot, forward, upload, or circulate child sexual abuse material. Do not ask a child to send it to you. If such material appears, stop interacting with it and report it through the appropriate official channel.

1 · Recognize patterns, not stereotypes

A single behavior does not prove abuse. Look at context and repeated boundary violations.

Boundary testing

Repeatedly asking a young person to keep secrets, hide conversations, or ignore a caregiver's safety rules.

Isolation

Pressuring them to move to private or disappearing channels, or discouraging them from talking to trusted adults.

Sexualized pressure

Requests for intimate images, sexual conversations, or attempts to normalize sexual behavior with a minor.

Coercion

Threats, blackmail, gifts used as leverage, or pressure to comply with demands.

These are warning signs to report—not a checklist for deciding guilt. Do not confront or publicly accuse someone based on suspicion.

2 · What to do when a concern appears

  1. Prioritize immediate safety. If someone is in immediate danger in the United States, call 911. Do not delay for a platform report.
  2. Use TikNation's report flow. Submit the account name, relevant platform link, approximate time, and a concise factual description.
  3. Preserve safely. Keep the original platform URL, account handle, timestamps, and report reference. Do not copy or redistribute illegal material.
  4. Stop direct engagement. Do not bait, threaten, warn, expose, or arrange a meeting with the suspected person.
  5. Escalate appropriately. In the United States, suspected online child exploitation can be reported to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children (NCMEC) CyberTipline.
Official U.S. reporting: NCMEC CyberTipline ↗. For immediate danger, call 911. For situations outside the United States, use the appropriate local child-protection or law-enforcement reporting channel.

3 · Protect privacy and evidence integrity

  • Record only what is necessary to explain the concern.
  • Keep reports factual: separate what you directly observed from what you infer.
  • Do not share a report in public chats, creator groups, social media, or with unrelated staff.
  • Never request identity documents, home addresses, passwords, or private case files from a young person.
  • Do not upload child sexual abuse material to TikNation or to this training page.
  • Use official reporting channels and follow their instructions about evidence preservation.
TikNation's advocate designation is not a law-enforcement credential and does not grant access to private messages, hidden user data, or moderation powers.
Core lesson

5 · Understand grooming and online exploitation

Online grooming is a process in which someone builds or misuses trust to manipulate a young person for exploitation. It may happen gradually or very quickly. It can begin in social media, livestream chats, direct messages, or online games. The person may be a stranger—or someone the young person already knows.

False identity and trust

Someone may lie about their age, identity, intentions, or relationship to the young person. A friendly profile or shared interests do not prove someone is safe.

Secrecy and isolation

They may encourage secret conversations, move chats to private apps, or tell the young person that trusted adults “wouldn't understand.”

Pressure and boundary crossing

They may introduce sexual topics, request intimate images, or push a young person to do things that feel uncomfortable.

Threats and sextortion

They may threaten to share an image, expose private information, or harm someone unless the young person sends money, more images, or obeys demands.

Important: A young person is never to blame for being manipulated, threatened, or exploited. Sending money or complying with demands does not guarantee the threats will stop. Encourage them to stop responding, preserve non-explicit account details and URLs where safe, get help from a trusted adult, and report. Never ask them to show or forward intimate material.
Core child-safety lesson

5A · What is a child predator?

A child predator is a commonly used term for an adult or other person who deliberately seeks to sexually exploit or abuse a child. This person may try to gain a child's trust, access, images, compliance, or silence. They may approach a child online or offline, and they may be a stranger, an acquaintance, or someone the child already knows.

Predatory behavior is about actions—not appearance. A profile photo, username, claimed age, popularity, gifts, or friendly manner cannot prove that someone is safe or unsafe. People who exploit children can use false identities, but they can also use their real identity and an existing relationship.

Building trust to gain access

Someone may give excessive attention, praise, gifts, money, game items, or promises of opportunities. They may try to become the child's “special” person or make the child feel they owe them something.

Secrecy and isolation

They may ask a child to hide messages, delete chats, use private or disappearing apps, keep a relationship secret, or stop talking to trusted adults.

Testing and crossing boundaries

They may introduce sexual topics, make inappropriate comments, ask personal questions, request intimate images, or gradually pressure a child to accept behavior that makes them uncomfortable.

Manipulation, threats, and blackmail

They may use guilt, affection, gifts, shame, threats, or blackmail to control a child. They may threaten to expose private information or images unless the child obeys.

Important things to understand

  • People who target children are not always strangers and do not all behave in the same way.
  • Any child can be targeted, regardless of gender, background, confidence, or online experience.
  • A child may feel affection, loyalty, fear, confusion, or shame and may not recognize manipulation immediately.
  • Accepting a gift, replying to a message, trusting someone, or sending something under pressure never makes exploitation the child's fault.
  • No single behavior proves that a person is a predator. Concerns should be assessed by trained safety professionals and appropriate authorities.
If a child may be targeted: Stay calm, listen without blame, and ask whether they are in immediate danger. Do not interrogate them, ask them to send or show explicit material, confront the suspected person, or try to run a sting. Use platform reporting and appropriate child-protection channels. In the United States, report suspected online child exploitation to the NCMEC CyberTipline. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

6 · What child trafficking means

Human trafficking involves exploiting a person through force, fraud, or coercion. When a person under 18 is induced to perform a commercial sex act, federal U.S. law treats it as child sex trafficking even if force, fraud, or coercion is not proven. Child trafficking can also involve forced labor or other forms of exploitation.

Trafficking is not always a stranger grabbing a child off the street. A trafficker may be someone the child knows, someone who pretends to be a romantic partner, or someone who uses promises of money, gifts, housing, work, travel, attention, or protection. Exploitation can occur online and offline, and a young person may not recognize what is happening or may feel unable to leave.

Warning signs that deserve a careful, supportive response

  • Someone else controls a young person's money, phone, identification, movements, or communications.
  • A young person seems afraid of a person who accompanies them or is not free to speak privately.
  • There are threats, debts, blackmail, or pressure connected to work, travel, housing, gifts, or sexual activity.
  • A person is being moved, recruited, or pressured into exploitative situations.

No single sign proves trafficking. Avoid assumptions based on clothing, neighborhood, identity, immigration status, or whether someone appears “cooperative.” Listen, prioritize safety, and refer concerns to trained authorities.

Do not investigate, interview repeatedly, promise rescue, or confront a suspected trafficker. Do not attempt to remove someone yourself. If there is immediate danger, contact emergency services.
Focused lesson · online exploitation

6A · What is online human trafficking?

Online human trafficking describes the ways people use the internet and digital tools to recruit, advertise, arrange, monitor, threaten, or exploit someone for forced labor or commercial sexual exploitation. The internet can be part of how trafficking is carried out; the exploitation may continue online, offline, or both.

Important distinction: Human trafficking does not require a person to be transported or taken across a border. Online grooming, online enticement, and sextortion can be serious forms of abuse or exploitation, but they are not automatically all the same crime as human trafficking. Trained authorities assess the facts. In the United States, a commercial sex act involving a person under 18 is treated as child sex trafficking under federal law, even when force, fraud, or coercion is not shown.

Recruitment through social platforms

A person may use direct messages, livestreams, gaming chats, or social networks to build trust, offer money or gifts, promise romance, or present a misleading opportunity. A relationship or offer that quickly demands secrecy, private contact, images, travel, or obedience deserves caution.

Fake jobs and opportunities

Fraudulent modeling, entertainment, travel, or employment offers may be used to lure someone into exploitation. Warning signs can include vague duties, pressure to act immediately, hidden costs or debts, demands to surrender documents, or attempts to isolate the person from trusted support.

Online advertising and coordination

Websites, social media, messaging services, and other digital channels can be misused to advertise exploitation, arrange commercial sexual activity, coordinate logistics, or connect buyers and exploiters. Do not search for, repost, or circulate suspected exploitative advertisements; report the concern.

Control through threats and dependency

Someone may use blackmail, threats to expose private information, debt, violence, control of money or identification, or threats against family to keep a person compliant. Gifts, money, or a romantic relationship do not make exploitation consensual.

Warning signs that may justify a safe report

  • An online contact offers unusually large payments, gifts, housing, travel, or work in exchange for secrecy or escalating demands.
  • Someone pressures a young person to move to private or disappearing messages, hide the relationship, or cut off trusted people.
  • A person is asked to create sexual content, meet someone, travel, recruit others, or perform work under threats or coercion.
  • Another person controls someone's earnings, accounts, identification, communications, movements, or access to help.
  • Threats, blackmail, fabricated debts, or fear are used to make someone continue an activity.

These are possible indicators—not a diagnostic checklist. One sign alone does not prove trafficking. Anyone can be affected, and victims may not identify themselves as victims.

What to do: Do not investigate, pose as a buyer or child, contact a suspected trafficker, arrange a meeting, follow anyone, or confront a person. If someone is in immediate danger in the United States, call 911. For suspected online child sexual exploitation, use the NCMEC CyberTipline. For suspected sex or labor trafficking and support referrals in the United States, contact the National Human Trafficking Hotline at 1-888-373-7888 or text 233733. Never request, download, upload, screenshot, forward, or circulate sexual images or videos of minors.

Trusted sources: DHS Know2Protect: key definitions ↗ · U.S. Department of Justice: child sex trafficking ↗ · NCMEC: online enticement ↗

7 · Protect yourself online: a practical safety plan

Keep personal details private

Do not share your home address, school, daily routine, live location, phone number, passwords, travel plans, or identifying details with people you do not know and trust offline.

Use strong account security

Use unique passwords, turn on multi-factor authentication, keep apps updated, and never share login codes—even with someone claiming to be platform support.

Check privacy settings

Limit who can message, tag, mention, view stories, join live sessions, or see your location. Review followers and connected apps regularly.

Trust your discomfort

You can say no, end a chat, block, and report. You do not owe anyone photos, secrets, money, a meeting, or an explanation.

  • Do not meet an online contact alone. Talk with a trusted adult and follow family safety rules.
  • Be cautious of offers that require secrecy, urgent decisions, travel, money transfers, or private images.
  • If someone threatens you, do not negotiate alone or send more material. Get help quickly.
  • If a friend tells you something worrying, listen calmly, believe them, avoid blame, and help them reach a safe adult.

Remember: Privacy tools help, but responsibility does not belong to children alone. Platforms, adults, and communities must build safer spaces and respond when concerns are raised.

Lesson 1

What is cyberbullying and online harassment?

Cyberbullying is bullying carried out through digital devices or online services—such as social media, messaging, gaming chats, livestreams, forums, or email. It can include repeated insults, threats, humiliating posts, rumor-spreading, exclusion, impersonation, or sharing private information to embarrass or intimidate someone.

Online harassment is unwanted online conduct that targets, intimidates, threatens, or repeatedly disturbs someone. It can include a single serious threat or a pattern of behavior. Not every rude disagreement is bullying, and the legal meaning of harassment varies by location; context, repetition, severity, and impact matter.

Common examples

  • Repeated abusive comments, messages, or unwanted contact.
  • Threats, intimidation, or coordinated pile-ons.
  • Impersonation, fake accounts, or posting private details (doxxing).
  • Sharing embarrassing images, edited media, or rumors to humiliate someone.
  • Targeted exclusion or harassment during online games and livestreams.

What it can do

It can cause fear, stress, isolation, reputational harm, missed school or work, and concerns for physical safety. A person being targeted is not responsible for someone else's abusive choices.

If you are targeted

Do not retaliate or amplify the content. Save relevant non-explicit messages, URLs, account names, and dates when safe; report the content and account; block or restrict the sender; tighten privacy settings; and tell a trusted adult, school, workplace, or support person as appropriate.

If you witness it

Do not like, repost, quote-post, or pile on. Offer private support, report the behavior, and seek help from a trusted adult or moderator. If there is a credible threat of violence or immediate danger, contact emergency services.

Evidence safety: Never screenshot, download, forward, upload, or circulate sexual images or videos of minors. Do not ask someone to send such material to prove a report. Preserve non-explicit account details and follow official reporting instructions.
Lesson 2

What is hacking?

Hacking is using technical knowledge to explore, test, or gain access to a computer, account, network, device, or digital service. The word itself does not always mean crime: permission, scope, intent, and what the person does with access are crucial.

  • Authorized security testing: a system owner gives written permission and defines what may be tested.
  • Unauthorized access: someone accesses or interferes with a system or account without permission. This can violate laws and cause real harm.
  • Account takeover: an attacker gains control of an account, often through stolen passwords, phishing, malware, or reused credentials.
A person claiming to be a “hacker,” platform employee, security researcher, or support agent does not automatically have permission to access your account. Never give anyone your password, one-time code, recovery phrase, or remote access because they ask.
Lesson 3

Different types of hackers

These labels describe common roles or motivations. They are not official job titles, and real-world behavior can be more complicated than a label.

White-hat / ethical hacker

Tests security with explicit authorization, follows agreed limits, and reports weaknesses responsibly.

Black-hat / malicious hacker

Uses unauthorized access or attacks for theft, extortion, disruption, spying, or other harmful purposes.

Grey-hat hacker

May find or test weaknesses without permission, even if they claim good intentions. Lack of permission still creates legal and safety risks.

Script kiddie

Uses tools or instructions made by others, often without deep understanding. Their actions can still cause serious harm.

Hacktivist

Claims a political or social cause as a motive for digital disruption, leaks, or unauthorized access.

State-sponsored actor

Operates with support, direction, or resources from a government, often for espionage or strategic disruption.

Cybercriminal group

Coordinates attacks such as fraud, data theft, ransomware, or extortion for financial or other gain.

Insider threat

A worker, contractor, or other trusted person misuses legitimate access—or exposes systems through careless actions.

About “red hat” and “blue hat”: These terms appear in some online explanations, but their meanings are inconsistent and they are not a dependable universal classification. Blue team usually means defenders; red team usually means authorized testers simulating attackers. Those are security-team roles, not necessarily types of people or permission to attack real systems.
Lesson 4

Common cyberattacks explained

Malware

Malicious software designed to spy, steal, damage, or take control. It can arrive through unsafe downloads, attachments, compromised websites, or deceptive apps.

Ransomware

Malware that locks or encrypts files or systems and demands payment. Paying does not guarantee recovery or deletion of stolen data.

Spyware

Software that secretly collects activity or information. Unexpected permissions, unknown apps, and device behavior changes deserve attention.

Password attacks

Attempts to guess, steal, or reuse passwords. Credential stuffing uses passwords exposed in other breaches; never reuse passwords between services.

Social engineering

Manipulating a person into revealing information, approving access, sending money, or bypassing a safety step. It often relies on urgency, fear, authority, or trust.

Denial-of-service (DoS/DDoS)

Flooding a service with traffic or requests so legitimate users cannot access it. Report outages to the service provider; do not attempt retaliation.

SIM swapping

Criminals trick a mobile carrier into moving a phone number to a SIM or device they control, potentially intercepting text codes. Add a carrier account PIN and use an authenticator or security key where available.

Session theft

An attacker steals or misuses a logged-in session so they may access an account without knowing its password. Sign out other sessions and secure the account if you suspect this.

Lesson 5

Phishing: the “fishing” scam 🤯

Phishing is a deception technique that tries to trick someone into clicking a harmful link, opening an unsafe attachment, revealing a password or code, or sending money. Messages may imitate a bank, delivery company, friend, creator, employer, or TikNation support.

Email phishing

A deceptive email sent to many people, often with a fake sign-in link, invoice, warning, or attachment.

Spear phishing

A targeted message tailored to a particular person using details about their role, interests, or contacts.

Whaling

A targeted scam aimed at executives or other high-value decision-makers.

Smishing

Phishing delivered by SMS or text message, often pretending to be a delivery, bank, or account alert.

Vishing

Voice or phone-call deception, including callers pretending to be support, a bank, or a relative.

QR phishing (quishing)

A QR code sends the person to a deceptive website or payment page. Check the destination before signing in or paying.

Clone phishing

A fake copy of a real message or notification, altered to replace a legitimate link or attachment with a malicious one.

Business email compromise

A criminal impersonates a boss, vendor, or partner to request a transfer, gift cards, payroll changes, or confidential information.

Warning signs: unexpected urgency; threats that an account will be closed; unusual payment requests; mismatched sender addresses or web domains; unexpected attachments; spelling or formatting oddities; requests for passwords or one-time codes. Modern scams can look polished, so spelling alone is not a reliable test.

Safe check: Do not use the message's link or phone number. Open the official app or type a known website address yourself, or contact the organization using a trusted number you already have.

Lesson 6

How to handle and recover from an attack

  1. Pause and disconnect from the scam. Do not reply, click again, pay, install remote-access software, or share a code. If a device appears actively compromised, disconnect it from Wi-Fi/mobile data if you can do so safely.
  2. Secure your email first. From a trusted, clean device, change the email password, enable multifactor authentication, check recovery details and forwarding rules, and sign out unknown sessions.
  3. Secure affected accounts. Use unique passwords, revoke unknown app connections and sessions, check account recovery settings, and contact the service through its official website or app.
  4. Protect your phone number and money. Contact your mobile carrier if you suspect SIM swapping. Contact your bank or card issuer immediately if money or payment details were involved.
  5. Update and scan devices. Install operating-system and app updates; use reputable built-in or trusted security tools. Get professional help if you cannot regain control.
  6. Preserve safe evidence. Keep message headers where available, sender details, account names, URLs, dates, transaction records, and report numbers. Do not forward malware or illegal content.
  7. Tell someone you trust. Young people should involve a trusted adult. A threat of violence, stalking, extortion, or immediate physical danger may need law-enforcement help.
Never “hack back,” threaten the sender, pay someone who promises to hack the attacker, or install a tool from an unsolicited helper. Recovery scammers often target people after an incident.

Additional lesson 1

Identity theft & protecting your identity

Identity theft happens when someone uses another person's personal information without permission, often to impersonate them, access accounts, obtain money or services, or commit fraud. Stolen information can include a name, address, date of birth, Social Security number, account credentials, or payment details.

How information gets stolen

Phishing, data breaches, stolen devices, unsafe websites, fake job or rental forms, malicious apps, and social engineering can expose personal details.

Warning signs

Unexpected password-reset notices, unfamiliar transactions, accounts you did not open, bills for services you did not request, or alerts about changes you did not make.

Protect yourself

Share the minimum information necessary, use unique passwords and multifactor authentication, secure your devices, monitor account alerts, and shred or securely dispose of sensitive documents.

If your identity is misused

Contact the affected bank or service using a trusted channel, secure affected accounts, review credit reports where relevant, and use the official U.S. recovery resource at IdentityTheft.gov.

Never post identity documents, full birth dates, home addresses, account numbers, passwords, or recovery codes in a public report or comment.
Additional lesson 2

Deepfakes, AI impersonation & synthetic media

Deepfakes are images, audio, or videos generated or altered with AI to make it appear that someone said or did something they did not. Scammers may clone a voice, fabricate an emergency call, impersonate a creator, or create fake evidence.

Warning signs

Unexpected urgent requests; unusual payment or secrecy demands; inconsistent details; suspicious account history; and a request that cannot be independently verified. Visual glitches and strange audio can occur, but convincing fakes may have no obvious flaws.

Verify out of band

Contact the person using a number or channel you already trust. Agree on a private family or team verification phrase for urgent requests. Do not rely on the incoming call, message, or link.

Non-consensual intimate deepfakes

Creating or sharing intimate synthetic images of a real person without consent can cause serious harm and may violate laws or platform rules. Do not repost, download, or circulate the material. Report it to the platform and seek trusted support.

AI-generated misinformation

Check the original source, date, context, and independent reporting before sharing a dramatic clip. A screenshot or video alone may not establish that an event happened.

Learn more: FTC guidance on AI voice-cloning scams · CISA Secure Our World.

Additional lesson 3

Digital footprints, privacy & doxxing

Your digital footprint is the trail of information created by your online activity: posts, comments, photos, likes, location clues, public profiles, and information others share about you. Deleting a post does not guarantee every copy disappears.

Location and routine clues

Live location tags, school or workplace details, event photos, visible street signs, and predictable posting patterns can reveal where someone is or when they are away.

Doxxing

Doxxing is publishing or threatening to publish someone's private identifying information without permission, often to intimidate or expose them. Do not repost it, even to criticize the person who posted it.

Privacy checkup

Review who can see posts, contact you, tag you, find you by phone or email, view followers, or access location. Remove unnecessary app permissions and old public information where possible.

Think before sharing

Ask whether a post reveals an address, school, work schedule, travel plan, identity document, or another person's private information. Get consent before posting others.

More help: FTC online privacy guidance.

Additional lesson 4

Online grooming, manipulation & romance scams

Some people use fake identities, affection, flattery, shared interests, gifts, or promises to build trust and then manipulate someone. Adults may be targeted by romance scams; children may face grooming and exploitation. The tactics can overlap, but the situations and reporting needs can differ.

Common manipulation tactics

Moving conversations off-platform, rushing intimacy, asking for secrecy, isolating someone from friends, inventing emergencies, requesting money or gift cards, or pressuring someone to share intimate content.

Romance scam warning signs

A person avoids live verification, claims to be far away, repeatedly has emergencies, asks for money or cryptocurrency, or wants access to bank accounts or packages.

Protect yourself

Keep conversations on-platform when possible, never send money or financial credentials to someone you have not independently verified, and talk to a trusted person before acting on urgent requests.

If a child is involved

Listen without blame, do not interrogate or confront the suspected person, do not arrange a meeting, and use the child-safety reporting guidance elsewhere in this Academy. Never request or circulate sexual images of minors.

Official resources: FTC romance scam guidance · NCMEC online enticement education.

Additional lesson 5

Extortion, sextortion & online blackmail

Extortion is using threats to pressure someone into paying, handing over information, or doing something against their will. Online blackmail may threaten to publish private messages, images, personal details, or fabricated material. Sextortion involves threats connected to sexual content.

Do not pay, negotiate, threaten, or send more material. Paying does not guarantee the threats will stop. Do not forward or repost intimate content. If a minor is involved, never request, save, download, or circulate sexual images or videos; use official reporting channels.
  1. Stop engaging and do not click further links or attachments.
  2. Preserve non-explicit evidence such as usernames, profile links, message URLs, dates, and payment demands.
  3. Report the account and content to the platform.
  4. Tell a trusted person. For minors, involve a trusted adult immediately.
  5. For U.S. online extortion or cybercrime, consider reporting to FBI IC3. For child sexual exploitation, use NCMEC CyberTipline. Call 911 for immediate danger.

Recovery scammers may promise to erase images, identify the offender, or hack the person for a fee. Do not trust unsolicited “recovery agents.”

Additional lesson 6

Phone, computer, Wi-Fi & network safety

Keep devices updated

Install operating-system, browser, and app updates from official settings or app stores. Updates often fix security weaknesses.

Install apps carefully

Use official app stores and the developer's genuine site. Review permissions, publisher information, and whether an app really needs access to contacts, microphone, camera, or location.

Public Wi-Fi

Use trusted networks when possible. Avoid sensitive transactions on networks you do not trust; use HTTPS sites and a reputable VPN if appropriate. Never accept unexpected certificate warnings.

Bluetooth and sharing

Turn off discoverability and sharing when not needed. Decline unknown pairing requests and do not accept files from strangers.

Backups

Keep important files backed up using a reputable cloud or offline backup. Protect backup accounts with multifactor authentication and test that recovery works.

Physical security

Use a screen lock, do not leave devices unattended, enable device-finding features, and know how to remotely lock or erase a lost device.

Trusted guide: CISA Secure Our World.

Additional lesson 7

Fake stores, giveaways, creator impersonation & investment scams

Fake online shops

Look-alike domains, copied product photos, unrealistic discounts, newly created accounts, and payment methods with little buyer protection can be warning signs. Search for independent reviews and verify the seller.

Fake giveaways

A scammer impersonates a creator or brand and says you won, then requests a fee, card details, password, or one-time code. Genuine prizes should not require your login credentials.

Creator impersonation

Check the exact account handle and official links. A display name or profile photo can be copied. Report impersonating profiles through the platform.

Investment and crypto scams

Guaranteed returns, pressure to act now, secret trading groups, fake celebrity endorsements, and demands to pay more to withdraw funds are major warning signs.

Report U.S. consumer fraud at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. If money or banking details were exposed, contact your financial institution immediately through a trusted number.

Additional lesson 8

What to do when your account is hacked

If you can still sign in, act quickly from a trusted device. If you cannot sign in, use the service's official account-recovery page—not a link sent by someone who contacted you.

  1. Secure your email first. Change its password, enable multifactor authentication, review recovery email and phone details, check forwarding rules, and sign out unfamiliar sessions.
  2. Change the affected password. Use a new, unique password. If you reused it elsewhere, change those passwords too.
  3. End unknown sessions. Use the account's security settings to sign out all devices and remove unfamiliar connected apps or access tokens where the service allows.
  4. Check account changes. Review email, phone number, profile details, payment methods, posts, messages, and recovery options for changes you did not make.
  5. Protect your phone number and finances. Contact your carrier if you suspect SIM swapping and your bank if payment details or money are affected.
  6. Warn contacts carefully. If the attacker sent messages from your account, let contacts know not to click links or send money. Do not share private evidence publicly.
  7. Report and document. Use the platform's official recovery and abuse-reporting process. Keep case numbers and non-sensitive records.
Never pay an unsolicited person who promises to recover your account, and never give them a password, one-time code, recovery phrase, or remote access.

New lesson

10 · AI, deepfakes and online impersonation

AI tools can make convincing fake profile photos, voices, messages, and videos. A familiar face or voice is not proof that a message is genuine. Images and recordings can be manipulated, taken out of context, or used to pressure someone.

Pause before trusting

Verify unexpected requests through a separate, known contact method. Do not use the phone number, link, or account supplied in the suspicious message.

Watch for urgent pressure

Requests for secrecy, money, gift cards, login codes, private images, or immediate action deserve caution—even when the voice or profile looks familiar.

Protect your likeness

Limit public personal photos and identifying details. Review privacy settings and ask trusted adults for help if someone misuses your image or voice.

Respond safely

Do not repost humiliating or intimate fake content. Save non-explicit URLs, account names, and timestamps where safe, report it, and seek trusted support.

For adults and advocates: Never create, request, or circulate sexualized synthetic content depicting a minor. Do not ask a young person to send intimate material to prove an allegation. Report the account and content through appropriate official channels.
New lesson

11 · Parent and guardian learning center

Children are more likely to ask for help when they expect calm support rather than punishment. Make online safety an ongoing conversation, not a one-time warning.

Start with connection

  • Ask open, non-judgmental questions about the apps, games, creators, and people your child interacts with.
  • Agree on family rules for messaging, livestreams, location sharing, downloads, and in-person meetups.
  • Tell them they can come to you after a mistake or uncomfortable interaction without being blamed.

Notice changes without jumping to conclusions

Sudden secrecy, fear, distress, unexplained gifts, or pressure from an online contact may justify a gentle conversation. None of these alone proves exploitation. Avoid accusations, threats, or taking away the child's only route to support.

If a child tells you something happened

  • Stay calm. Say: “Thank you for telling me. You are not in trouble. This is not your fault.”
  • Ask whether they are in immediate danger. Do not repeatedly question them or conduct your own investigation.
  • Do not view, save, forward, or request explicit images. Preserve non-explicit account details and URLs where safe.
  • Use the platform's reporting tools and contact the appropriate child-protection authority. Call emergency services for immediate danger.

Young people · age-appropriate learning

Teen Safety Hub

You deserve to feel safe online. You can stop a conversation, leave a livestream, block an account, and ask for help—even if you started the conversation, accepted a gift, shared information, or made a mistake. You are not to blame if someone manipulates or threatens you.

When someone asks for secrecy

A person who insists you hide a friendship, move to private chats, or keep messages from trusted adults may be testing your boundaries. Talk to a safe adult.

When someone asks for images

You never owe anyone an intimate image. Do not send more if pressured. Do not forward or show intimate images of minors to friends or helpers. Tell a trusted adult and use safe reporting channels.

When someone offers gifts or money

A gift does not create a debt. Be cautious if someone expects secrecy, private contact, images, a meeting, or obedience in return.

When you need help

Choose an adult who listens and helps you feel safe—a parent, guardian, school counselor, teacher, or another trusted adult. If the first person does not listen, tell another safe adult.

Quick plan: Stop replying if safe, block/report the account, tell a trusted adult, and seek emergency help if you are in immediate danger. You do not have to handle it alone.

Urgent support guide

Sextortion: if someone is threatening you

You are not to blame. Threats and blackmail are the other person's responsibility. You do not have to pay, send more images, or keep the threat secret.
  1. Get support now. Tell a trusted adult or safe person. If there is immediate physical danger, call emergency services.
  2. Do not comply with demands. Paying or sending more material does not guarantee the threats will stop.
  3. Stop engaging when safe. Do not threaten, negotiate, retaliate, or arrange to meet the person.
  4. Keep non-explicit details. Where safe, note usernames, profile links, URLs, dates, times, and threatening messages. Do not download, screenshot, forward, upload, or share sexual images of anyone under 18.
  5. Report and get specialist help. Use the platform's report tools and appropriate official channels. In the U.S., NCMEC's CyberTipline accepts reports of suspected online child exploitation.

For help removing or limiting the spread of explicit images taken when someone was under 18, NCMEC provides Take It Down. Use the official service's instructions; do not upload or send the image to TikNation or another person. The service uses a hash generated on the user's device.

For immediate danger in the United States, call 911. For suspected trafficking, the National Human Trafficking Hotline is 1-888-373-7888 or text 233733. These are external services, not TikNation support lines.

Youth protection standard

Youth Protection & Privacy Standard

TikNation should collect the minimum information needed to receive and review a safety report, restrict access to authorized staff, and explain how information is handled in plain language.

  • Minimize: Do not ask young people to provide passwords, identity documents, home addresses, explicit material, or unrelated private details.
  • Limit access: Report details should be visible only to staff with a legitimate safety role and appropriate permissions.
  • Protect reporters: Do not reveal a reporter's identity to unrelated users or permit retaliation.
  • Be transparent: The platform's privacy notice should explain what is collected, why, who can access it, retention practices, and how to request help.
  • Handle fairly: Reports are allegations, not findings. Review them consistently and do not automatically punish an account solely because a report was filed.
  • Escalate responsibly: Use official channels for emergencies and suspected exploitation; do not conduct vigilante investigations.

Staff learning

14 · Moderator response training

Moderators should respond consistently, protect privacy, and escalate concerns through approved internal routes. They are not investigators and should not attempt to determine guilt.

Receive a report calmly

Thank the reporter, avoid blame, do not promise absolute secrecy, and check whether anyone appears to be in immediate danger. Do not ask for explicit material or unnecessary personal details.

Record only relevant facts

Capture the reported account identifier, content URL or platform reference, approximate time, report category, and a concise description of what was observed. Distinguish direct observations from assumptions. Keep access limited to authorized staff.

Escalate—do not investigate

Use TikNation's approved moderation escalation process. Do not create decoy accounts, impersonate minors, message a suspected offender, arrange meetings, conduct public callouts, or ask community members to investigate.

Handle illegal material safely

Never download, screenshot, forward, upload, or circulate child sexual abuse material. Never ask a reporter or child to send it. Stop viewing/interacting and follow official reporting instructions. Do not put explicit material into internal notes or chat.

Use fair, proportionate moderation

A report is an allegation, not proof. Follow platform policy, preserve due process, document the moderation rationale, and do not impose automatic penalties solely because a report was submitted. Escalate urgent risks promptly.

Protect the reporter and affected young person

Do not reveal who reported, share case details with unrelated staff, or make promises about outcomes. Offer the platform's reporting and support routes, and refer emergencies to emergency services.

This training does not authorize moderators to access private information beyond their assigned platform permissions or to act as law enforcement, social workers, or forensic examiners.
